## Review
- Is allocation A better than B?
	- Allocation A __Pareto Dominates__ allocation B if nobody prefers B and at least someone prefers A
- Possible to yield a Pareto Improvement if: $$MRS^B>MRS^A$$
- But this can't happen after buying and selling as: $$MRS^A=\frac{p_1}{p_2}=MRS^B$$

## For each good, holding its quantity constant: the cost of goods can't be decreased by reallocating quantities across firms

- Suppose two firms are producing $q_1$ and $q_2$ of the same good
	- Their marginal costs are $mc_1(q_1)$ and $mc_2(q_2)$
	- Total quantity is $Q=q_1+q_2$
- Could we switch a unit from one firm to the other and reduce costs?
	- Slightly reduce the quantity of firm 2 and increase that of firm 1?
	- This keeps total quantity constant
- What's the change in total cost? $$-mc_2(q_2)+mc_1(q_1)=0$$
	- Because the firms will have chosen $mc(q)=p$
## Second Welfare Theorem
- Any given Pareto efficient allocation in a perfectly competitive market is a general equilibrium outcome for some initial allocation of resources

### Case Example: Feeding America
- Q: someone gives you 300 million pounds of food to distribute to 100s of food banks around the US

## Market Power
- A firm in perfect competition has no effective control over the price that it charges
- A firm with control over price has Market Power
- Most extreme form of market power is monopoly

## Sources of Market Power
1. Switching Costs
	- Sometimes it is costly for a customer to switch products
	- Loyalty benefits, network goods, time/hassle cost of switching
2. Product differentiation
	- Differences in products (real or perceived) leads to market power
3. Regulation
	- Sometimes government regulations lead to market power
	- Publicized industry
4. Sole control over an input of production
	- DeBeers Diamonds, Standard Oil
5. Extreme Economies of Scale
	- In the case of very high fixed costs and very low variable costs

## Key Difference Between a Firm in PC and one with Market Power.
- Firm in a perfectly competitive market faces no relationship between the quantity it decides to produce and the price.
	- Firm can double q and sell at the same price
	- How do we reconcile this with the Law of Demand?
- Monopolists however, face a downward-sloping demand curve
- To sell double the quantity, prices have to fall.

## Marginal Revenue
- Assumptions here: no "Price Discrimination"
	- Firms can't charge different prices to different consumers
- Suppose a monopoly is selling at $Q_1$ at $P_1$
- Then suppose it wants to increase to $Q_1 + 1$
	- Must reduce $P$ to $P_2$
- How does this affect the firm's revenue?
	- Good news (for monopolist) - it sells an additional unit (gains $P_2$)
	- Bad news (for monopolist) - all previous units are now cheaper (loses $\Delta p \times Q_1$)

## Marginal Revenue
$$MR(Q)=P_2+\frac{\Delta P}{\Delta Q}\times Q_1$$

### Marginal Revenue: an example with linear demand
Given an inverse demand curve: $$P=a-bQ$$
Slope: $\frac{\Delta P}{\Delta Q}=-b$
Marginal Revenue: $$\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
MR(Q)&=P+\frac{\Delta P}{\Delta Q}\times Q\\
&=a-bQ+\frac{\Delta P}{\Delta Q}\times Q\\
&=a-bQ+(-bQ)\\
&=a-2bQ
\end{split}
\end{equation}$$

## Normative Analysis of Market Outcomes
- How should we analyze whether market outcomes are "good"?
- Concept of Pareto Dominance/Pareto Efficiency can be helpful
- Hard to agree on what is a good, maybe easier to agree on what is better
- Definition: An allocation of resources; what firms produce, how they produce these products, who consumes what
- Allocation of resources B "Pareto dominates" allocation A if in moving from A to B
	- No one is worse off
	- At least one person is better off
- Visually, allocations Pareto Dominate if they are to the northeast of another allocation ![[images/pareto A.png]]
- A Pareto Frontier: ![[images/pareto frontier.png]]
- Allocation is Pareto Efficient if no feasible allocation Pareto Dominates it
- Pareto Inefficient = not Pareto Efficient
	- Captures a notion of wasted potential benefit
	- Hard to argue in favor of any allocation that is Pareto inefficient
	- Note it is a notion of efficiency; there should be no waste
		- No forgone opportunities for trade
		- No wasted production opportunities
		- No wasted inputs
- NB: this concept has no concern about equity

## Pareto Efficiency/Dominance Subtlety
- For every Pareto inefficient allocation, there is a Pareto Efficient allocation preferred by everyone
- Not every Pareto Efficient allocation Pareto dominates every Pareto inefficient allocation

## Does Perfect Competition lead to Pareto Efficient Outcomes?
- PE is a very useful (albeit limited) concept for evaluating market outcomes
- Would the outcome be PE if all markets are perfectly competitive?
	- The First Welfare Theorem of Economics says yes

## The First Welfare Theorem of Economics
- Def: an allocation of resources = what firms produce, how they produce these products, who consumes what
- Claim: the allocation of resources in a perfectly competitive equilibrium is Pareto Efficient
- Important: "perfectly competitive"
	- Large number of buyers and sellers (no strategic behavior)
	- No externalities or other "market failures"
- More specifically:
	- Holding all quantities constant, there is no Pareto-Improving trade between consumers
	- Two people (A and B) have chosen consumption of two goods in a market place
	- Can we do the following, such that A is indifferent and B is better off?
		- Take $\Delta x_1 = 1$ from person A. Give it to B
		- Take some quantity $\Delta x_2$ from person B. Give it to A.
	- For A to be indifferent, we must have: $$MU_1^A\Delta{x_1}+MU_2^A\Delta{x_2}=0$$
	- And: $$\Delta x_2 = \frac{MU_1^A}{MU_2^A}$$
	- What would B's change in Utility be? $$MU_1^B\Delta{x_1}+MU_2^B\Delta{x_2}=\Delta{U^B}$$
	- Or, using $\Delta x_1 = 1$ and the $\Delta x_2$ we found above: $$\Delta U^B=MU_1^B-MU_2^B MRS^A$$ 
	- Does this make B better off?
		- Yes, if: $\Delta U^B>0$
		- Or, equivalently, $\frac{MU_1^B}{MU_2^B}>MRS^A$
		- Or, finally: $$MRS^B>MRS^A$$
- Prices do the work of bartering
- We know Consumer A chooses $x_1^A$ and $x_2^A$, and Consumer B chooses $x_1^B$ and $x_2^B$ such that: $$MRS^A=MRS^B=\frac{p_1}{p_2}$$
- This means that the MRS is equalized across people
- So, does this trade make B better off?
	- WTS $MRS^B>MRS^A$
	- But, we know in a world of perfect competition, $MRS^A=MRS^B$
	- So, by contradiction, there is no Pareto-Improving trade between consumers in a world of perfect competition $\blacksquare$

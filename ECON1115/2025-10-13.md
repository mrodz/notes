Two approaches to optimal firm choice of output with one input

Firms choose inputs optimally such that: 
$$pMP_L(L^*)=w$$
And choose outputs optimally such that:
$$p=MC(Q^*)$$

An isoquant is a curve representing combinations of inputs that allow a firm to make a particular quantity of output.

An isocost line shows all input combinations that yield the same cost:
$$C=rK+wL$$

The Firm's Cost Function: Graphical Approach
$$TC(w,r,Q)=wL^*(w,r,Q)+rK^*(w,r,Q)$$
## Slope of the Isoquant
How to calculate $\frac{\Delta K}{\Delta L}$ along the isoquant? It must be that:
$$MP_K\Delta{X}+MP_L\Delta{L}=0$$
or, rearranging:
$$\frac{\Delta{K}}{\Delta{L}}=-\frac{MP_L}{MP_K}$$
This ratio is the "Marginal Rate of Technical Substitution"

### Optimal Consumption Rule
$$MU_1=MU_2\times\frac{p_1}{p_2}$$
### Cost minimization requirement
$$\frac{MP_L}{MP_K}=\frac{w}{r}$$
At minimum cost, marginal rate of technical substitution equals the ratio of input prices
Alternative: $$\frac{MP_L}{w}=\frac{MP_K}{r}$$
If the wage rate is super high, the firm will increase the marginal quality of labor
If we have diminishing returns, the firm will decrease number of workers.

## Costs
### How does total cost change when $Q$ increases?
Suppose a pizza joint has 3 ovens and 10 staff to produce 300 pizzas daily. Now, suppose it doubles ovens to 6 and doubles staff to 20.

How many pizzas will it produce?
1. =600?
2. >600?
3. <600?

All three could happen. It depends.

### Returns to scale
When inputs double, output doubles too
- This means we have **Constant Returns to Scale**
When inputs double, output more than doubles
- This means we have **Increasing Returns to Scale**
When inputs double, output less than doubles
- This means we have **Decreasing Returns to Scale**

## Shape of Marginal Cost Curves
We've assumed that Marginal Costs always increase
- 2nd unit more expensive than 1st, 3rd more expensive than 2nd, etc.
- Justified by the assumption of Diminishing returns kicks in immediately
- Economists usually assume MC initially falls, but eventually starts rising

Why this shape?
- Key reason is that specialization is very important
- But at some point, gains from specialization exhausted

## Fixed and Variable Costs
Fixed costs are costs which do not change as quantity of output changes
Variable costs are costs which do change as quantity of output changes
Total costs are equal to the sum of fixed and variable costs

## Average Costs
The average cost of producing $Q$
$$AFC=\frac{FC}{Q}$$
$$AVC=\frac{VC}{Q}$$
$$ATC=\frac{TC}{Q}=AFC+AVC$$
![[images/Pasted image 20251013140303.png]]

Typical average total cost curve is U-shaped for two distinct reasons:
1. Average fixed costs slope downwards
2. Typical average variable cost curve are U-shaped
For low $Q$ these go in the same direction
But at some point, increasing $AVC$ will push up $AC$ in spite of (1)

## Relationship between Marginal and Average Cost
- If Marginal Costs are greater than Average Costs then AC are <u>rising</u>
- If Marginal Costs are less than Average Costs then AC are <u>falling</u>
- So when MC is equal to AC, then Average Costs are at a <u>minimum</u>

## Accounting Costs and Economic Costs
- Accounting costs are the direct costs of operating a business
- Economic costs are the sum of these accounting costs and opportunity costs

Suppose person A drives an Uber full time
Annual costs:
- Gas: $5,000
- Car Lease: $8,000
- Car Maintenance: $2,000
- Insurance: $3,000
Before Uber, they were a Chef, and could earn $25,000 in a restaurant. Driving Uber means this is no longer possible.

## The over-arching puzzle
- Was failed growth due to luck, policy, institutions, or Latin America's place in the international system?

## Underdevelopment and Dependency
- "Historical legacies" provide one reason for lagging Latin American development, but another is its unique place in the world economic system
	- "Underdeveloped" countries are not following in the steps of countries that industrialized later
	- As "late developers," Latin American countries entered the world economy on the terms of wealthier nations
	- Did these terms relegate Latin America to a poverty trap?

## Capitalism and Underdevelopment
- Rich countries produce capital-intensive industrial goods and poor countries export raw materials and agricultural products
- World Systems Theorists: core or center countries set the rules of the game, and periphery countries are forced to play as pawns.
- "Rich countries become rich by making other countries poor" (Franko, pg. 60)

## Japan vs. Latin America
- Mid 19th century: Japan was a poor, economically isolated "backwater" of the world economy
- Meiji Restoration: centralizes and modernizes economy, setting the stage for rapid industrialization and development
- By 1905, Japan had grown powerful enough to defeat a European power (Russia) in a war

Why was this path of development available to Japan but not to Latin American countries???

## Coming into the 20th century
- Weak states after independence (reprise)
	- Local elites (caudillos) resist giving up local power
	- Recurrent civil wars undermine state building
	- Trade- and market-led state development led to horizontal negotiations with peripheries (vs. hierarchical incorporation in Western Europe)
	- Economic elites retain considerable power
- Late 19th century: export boom
	- Falling costs of trade, growing market for food and commodities
	- Economic growth but highly uneven
	- Increase in land concentration
	- High dependence on single exports in many countries
	- Uneven pattern of development

## Latin America as a Periphery
- Latin America began as a colony, which structured its entry into the international system
	- Economic and political institutions focused on rapid wealth extraction
- Industrialization came late due to resource endowment but also due to institutional legacy
	- Trapped LaTam in position of exporting raw materials
- Local elites benefited from the system and blocked attempts at change from within

## The Prebisch/Singer Hypothesis
- Peripheral countries export natural resources, which traps them in a state of underdevelopment
	- As the world develops, consumers spend a smaller percentage of their income on food
	- Engel's law: declining terms of trade for agriculture/resource exporters
- Interacts with power and politics to perpetuate global inequality
	- As the center develops, wages rise, and consumers demand more industrial goods
	- Patterns of demand shaped by corporations, elite economic interests
	- The periphery needs to sell more to keep up in the face of falling demand, but center benefits when prices for resources are kept low

 ## Vicious and Virtuous Circles of Development
- Dependence theory: Countries that start in the periphery tend to stay there
	- Declining terms of trade keep them poor
	- Bc. they are poor -> unable to industrialize
	- Bc. they are unable to industrialize -> laggard

## Hirschman's Key Ideas
- Development is constrained by bottlenecks that prevent resources from going where they are most needed.
	- A country has lots of labor, but investors can't build a textile factory because they lack technology and roads
- Breaking up bottlenecks can have positive feedback effects up and down the supply chain.
	- Backward linkages: new textile factory stimulates growth of thread manufacturers, wool producers, machine makers, etc.

## Enter stage: The State
- Jump starting the development process may require a big up-front investment or push to build several capacities simultaneously
	- Usually too costly for a private company or person to undertake alone
- The state can play an important role in getting the ball rolling
	- Only domestic actor with enough resources and insurance to make costly/risky investment
	- Can adopt policies that ease the transition
- Import-Substitution Industrialization

## What is Import-Substitution Industrialization (ISI)?
- Basic idea: foster development through inward-looking economic policies to prioritize industrialization
- Typically consisted of a portfolio of policies oriented toward:
	- Protectionism of various types
	- State intervention in the economy

## Protectionism
- Use economic policy to privilege domestic industry over international competitors
- Tools:
	- Tariffs, quotas, licensing, manipulating the exchange rate, subsidies, etc.
	- Targeted consumer goods in most Latin American countries
	- Incentivized multinational corporations to invest in local industries to get around high barriers to trade
- Force multinationals to work with local industries, facilitating resource and skill transfers
	- BUT: allowed uncompetitive industries to thrive, created distortions

## State involvement in the economy
- Increase in state-owned or mixed public-private enterprises in Latin America
	- Especially prominent in heavy industry, mining, infrastructure, oil, etc.
	- Think of Venezuela's petrol!!!!
- SOE's receive funding from state and various advantages
	- Public funds for R&D
	- Insurance for foreign investors
	- Preferential access to capital, credit, etc.
- Challenges:
	- Political capture, crowding out private companies, inefficiency and corruption

## Did ISI work?
- Most countries increased production of basic goods and some heavy industry
- Levels of growth varied considerably, as did import dependence
- Case study: Mexico
	- High tariffs meant to "Mexicanize" industry and investment
		- Benefits:
			- increasing in living standards, literacy, health
			- Major industrialization and urbanization
		- Drawbacks:
			- Distortionary policies, dependence on government spending
			- Political power of economic elites distorts public finance priorities

## Why did ISI end?
- Though many countries experienced growth in the early ISI period, results began to lag by the 1970s
- As growth slowed, became more difficult to sustain ISI policies
	- To pay for expensive subsidies and populist programs
- Eventually, forced to undertake structural adjustment programs and open to free trade

## Latin America and the Debt Crisis
- ISI policies required initial borrowing to "jump-start" the development process
	- Infrastructure
	- Part of theory is that private actors will not be able to undertake this investment alone
- External factors
	- Efforts to combat US inflation in the early 80's raised the costs of debt-financed policies
		- Real interest rates increased, domestic capital moved abroad
		- Recession drove down demand for Latin American products
	- Countries became unable/unwilling to service their debt
		- Impacts on banks as well as other countries
		- IMF called in to bail out countries, but arguably mis-diagnose problem
- Difficult adjustments
	- IMF provided financial support, but it came with conditionality
		- Devalue currency
		- Reduce domestic spending
		- Eliminate subsidies
		- Privatize state owned enterprise
	- Proponents:
		- IMF implemented needed changes that governments were unwilling or unable to address

## Privatization
- Under pressure from debt, macroeconomic and political crises, virtually all countries in Latin America began to liberalize
	- Example of the "Asian Economic Miracle"
- Sharp reduction in tariffs

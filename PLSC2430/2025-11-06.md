## Example 1: Market Reforms
- Many openings for corruption under ISI/state-led development
- Market reforms opened new opportunities for personal enrichment
- High-profile cases of corruption during privatization in 1980's/1990's damage the reputation of reforms/reformers
- Hard to pinpoint a single effect on "corruption"

## Example 2: Democratization
- Autocratic politics enabled and encouraged corruption in Latin America
- Democratization may enhance political accountability, but also new incentives for corruption
	- Shadow of the future: worry of being removed from office encourages graft/networking while in office

## Clientelism and Democracy in Latin America
- Clientelism: "the proffering of material goods in return for electoral support, where the criterion of distribution that the patron uses is simply: did you/will you support me?"
- Pork barrel politics: target a specific group of society for political gain (at the expense of others)

## Vote or Turnout Buying
- Turnout buying: offering resources in exchange for turning out to vote or not
- Vote buying: offering resources in exchange for voting in a particular way

## Incidence of Vote and Turnout purchasing
- Often considered to be a normal part of the political process
- Slippage in what counts as vote buying
	- Does the promised transfer need to be an individual, or can it be made collectively (to communities or certain groups?)

## Patronage and the Public Sector
- Patronage: offering public resources or jobs in exchange for past and future political support
- Often sustained through repeated interactions, reciprocity

## Public programs and political corruption
- Beyond personalist ties, delivery of public goods and services itself can be manipulated for political gain

## Patronage and Political Competition
- Creates incentive to offer continuous support to incumbent politicians
	- Collective action problem: public might benefit from removing the incumbent, but the first mover will lose access to the goods and removal efforts might not succeed
- Reduces effectiveness of party competition; damages democracy

## Why is clientelism so prevalent in Latin America?
- Empirical link between poverty and clientelism
	- Also noticed in South Asia, Africa, 18th century England, 1950's Italy, etc

## Role of Institutions
- Large literature examining how electoral or political institutions shape incentives for clientelism
	- Single vs multi-member electoral districts
	- Proportional representation vs. "first past the post"
	- Open vs. closed-list PR
	- Electoral technology
- Most democracies ban or restrict vote buying and patronage, but these laws are only sporadically enforced

## A culture of corruption?
- The prevalence of corruption and clientelism in Latin America can be thought of as a political equilibrium
	- Actors know that corruption and clientelism are widespread
	- This shifts expectations and perceptions about what to expect from others in society

## Can the cycle be broken?
- There have been many efforts to combat corruption in Latin America
	- Some focus on institutional reforms
	- Some focus on culture/public opinion
	- Undertaken by different actors: top-down vs. bottom-up reforms, external actors
- Yet, reforms are often more symbolic than material in impacts often due to a lack of political will
